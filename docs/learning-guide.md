# Learn the business behind each round

The campaign is a practice environment for operating judgment. The market, product costs, shocks, and coaching targets are synthetic game assumptions. Historical CNH disclosures provide context for the problems an equipment company faces; they do not calibrate this simulation or make it a forecast of CNH.

## Know what the board expects

Start with the board mandate, before changing the operating plan. The opening scenario determines the emphasis: a soft market calls for cash discipline and protection of the franchise; a flat market calls for profitable share and a stronger business; an upswing calls for capturing demand without overextending. New campaigns disclose monthly targets, quarterly milestones, and annual commitments from the start. The public outlook can change the board's advice, but it does not rewrite those targets after a bad month. An older save that never showed this mandate uses a labeled retrospective learning benchmark; it is not treated as a promise the player previously made.

The board assesses these realized measures:

| Measure | Weight | What the player should investigate |
|---|---:|---|
| Earned company value | 30 | Has risk-adjusted book equity improved after all recorded obligations and realization discounts? |
| Cash plus available corporate credit | 15 | Can the company fund its commitments without mistaking borrowed cash for new resources? |
| Factory inventory cover | 10 | Is money tied up in stock beyond the recent shipment run rate? |
| Dealer delivery | 20 | Are replenishment orders being filled? |
| First-pass quality | 10 | Are assembled machines accepted without rework? |
| Retail unit market share | 15 | Is the company winning customer sales relative to the modeled market? |

An exact target earns **75/100 attainment points**, weighted by its importance; the disclosed stretch level earns **100**. Meeting the operating commitment is deliberately different from exceptional performance. The total is **Meeting** at 70–84, **Beating** at 85 or above, and **Behind** below 70. These status bands coexist with the A–F score scale. The review shows each actual value, target, and gap, so the total is not the only feedback.

Quarterly reviews use the whole quarter's sales, orders, and assemblies for operating rates, with the quarter-end financial position. Earned value is cumulative from the opening company; it is not a fresh profit target that resets each quarter. Financial distress, negative closing cash, and severely inadequate customer service impose disclosed score ceilings. No assembly earns no measured quality result, and idle cash cannot substitute for serving customers.

The board's value measure is a teaching adjustment to book equity, not a valuation at which the company could be sold. Capacity and technology can support future earnings, but their budgets do not become an automatic investment return or score bonus. See the [board mandate](board-mandate.md) for the exact target schedule, tolerances, and guardrails.

## Know what a sale earns

Start with three different questions:

| Question | Measure | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Does another shipment help pay the bills? | Unit contribution after manufacturing cost and modeled warranty provision | Whether the sale adds money toward the rest of the business's expenses. |
| Can the whole company cover its costs this month? | Company break-even at the planned sales mix | Whether expected contribution covers the modeled shared costs and other income or expenses. |
| Will enough cash remain to operate? | Cash forecast and balance sheet | Whether inventory, collections, customer lending, investment, and debt payments leave enough liquidity. |

A positive unit contribution does not establish company profitability. Company profitability does not establish cash availability. Producing a machine creates inventory; revenue is recognized when it ships to a dealer. Selling out of dealer stock reaches a customer but does not create a second manufacturer equipment sale.

The price control is the **customer list price in thousands of dollars**. The manufacturer receives **86% of that price** on a shipment to a dealer. For the opening tractor, a $145,000 list price means $124,700 of manufacturer equipment revenue. Standard materials and conversion cost $88,000, leaving $36,700 before warranty provision and shared expenses. A $10,000 list-price discount reduces manufacturer revenue by $8,600 per shipment; additional volume has to recover that lost contribution.

The tractor's standard variable-cost floor is approximately **$102,326 of list price**, calculated as $88,000 divided by 86%. Its warranty-adjusted floor also covers the modeled warranty provision. Both exclude shared company expenses. Overtime, rework, and inventory carrying stay in common monthly expenses, so the displayed contribution is a teaching measure rather than every marginal cash cost.

The allocated full-cost price assigns common costs by each product's share of wholesale revenue, then spreads that allocation over its shipments. This product allocation excludes loan interest income and borrowing interest. It is not a uniquely correct product cost or a reason by itself to abandon a product. Neither floor nor allocated price is automatically the right commercial price. Raising price may improve contribution per machine while reducing demand. Lowering price may bring more demand than the factory can supply, or reduce margin without enough additional sales.

Treat a break-even volume as a planning reference at the displayed prices, costs, and product mix. It does not promise those orders exist, that capacity is available, or that costs stay fixed as output changes. If contribution is nonpositive, simply increasing sales at the same economics cannot cover positive shared costs.

Company operating break-even credits loan interest income; pretax break-even also covers borrowing interest. A positive margin of safety means projected wholesale revenue exceeds the pretax break-even revenue at that mix. A negative value is the shortfall. With no shipments or nonpositive combined contribution, the threshold is unavailable rather than an invented zero. See the [unit-economics definitions](unit-economics.md) for the full bridge to the accounts.

## Understand the customer lending rate

The starting **7.25% customer APR** is the annual rate offered on new equipment-buyer loans. It is not the manufacturer's own borrowing rate. The game separates three rates:

| Rate | Applies to | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Customer APR, chosen in Decisions | New 24-month customer equipment loans | Affects demand and lending now; the new loan first earns interest next month. |
| 7.50% corporate funding APR | Opening corporate debt | A new draw or repayment changes cash and debt now; the interest effect follows next month. |
| 5.50% customer-finance funding APR | Opening matched finance debt | Funding follows the loan book; its interest expense follows the opening funding balance. |

Existing customer loans keep the APR at which they were originated. Lowering today's offer does not reprice that book. At 7.25%, the first month's interest on an unchanged $100,000 opening loan balance is about **$604.17**, with about **$4,166.67** of scheduled principal under the simplified equal-principal, 24-month model. Interest declines with the outstanding balance. Principal collections reduce the loan asset; only interest is revenue.

At the neutral rate, 32% of retail sales use the manufacturer's financing and each financed customer borrows 80% of list price. One percentage point less APR adds 0.012 to the demand multiplier and 1.4 percentage points to that financed share, within the model's bounds. Actual sales still need available equipment. More financing may support sales while using more company cash and creating more credit exposure.

Matched finance debt funds 80% of the customer loan book. A new $100,000 loan therefore uses $20,000 of company funds before collections and other activity, alongside $80,000 of matched borrowing. The reported net finance funding change also reflects older loans amortizing and credit losses. Customer APR minus 5.50% is not a complete finance margin: it ignores the company's cash contribution, losses, corporate funding, and the manufacturing effects of changed demand.

Use **Customer financing** to compare lower, current, and higher rates under the same plan and elapsed months. The first projected month's loan-interest revenue is the same because it comes from existing loans. Look further ahead for the interest effect of new lending, then assess total profit and cash together. See the [financing guide](financing-guide.md) for the engine-backed explanation.

## Follow the full monthly statements

The monthly report shows the full balance sheet: cash, receivables, owned inventory, net fixed assets, customer loans, both debt balances, payables, warranty reserves, and equity. Each row compares opening with closing and gives the movement. The accompanying income statement and cash bridge connect operations to those balances.

Green and red are prompts to inspect a change, not automatic strategic verdicts. An increase in cash can come from a loan. A decline in debt can drain the operating cushion. More inventory can protect delivery or signal overproduction; more receivables can reflect growth or cash tied up with dealers. These ambiguous balances remain neutral rather than receiving an invented good/bad judgment.

Check three relationships: assets equal liabilities plus equity; net profit changes equity; cash changes through operations, investment, lending, and funding. A warranty provision is an expense and reserve addition; a paid claim normally uses cash and that reserve. Equipment inventory is an asset until shipment releases its standard cost into cost of goods sold. The [factory and cost ledger](factory-diagnostics.md) helps trace those stages.

## Use a deliberate monthly planning loop

1. **Read the assignment and opportunity.** Check the next published board targets, the year's market direction, next-month demand change, dealer inventory, and actual share. Industry demand, company requests, dealer orders, shipments, and retail sales answer different questions.
2. **Check the economics.** Compare net manufacturer price with contribution and company break-even. Separate inventory assets, recurring expenses, and cash commitments. If changing customer APR, inspect both sales demand and the financing consequences.
3. **Find the physical constraint.** Use factory diagnostics to check controllers, incomplete units, rework, capacity, and existing stock. Extra output does not create demand, and extra capacity does not create missing components.
4. **Check the cash path.** Inspect the balance sheet and projections. Inventory and lending can absorb cash while profit improves. Borrowing raises cash and debt together; it does not create operating profit or additional cash-plus-credit resources.
5. **Compare an investment and an operating alternative.** Save the plan, test a targeted investment, and check its delay, recurring cost, and expected benefit. Then reconsider prices or production if needed. A capacity project may need a revised output plan to use its slots; engineering may create demand the factory cannot yet satisfy.
6. **Commit and explain.** Review the board's actual-versus-target assessment, the recorded forecast, the full statements, and the separate decision coach. At each quarter-end, inspect the milestone over the whole quarter. Pick one lesson to test in the next month.

## Read coaching without learning the wrong lesson

Decision quality and outcome luck are separate. A sensible plan can meet a weak market or a supply disruption; an aggressive plan can benefit from an unexpectedly strong month. A coaching grade should be read alongside its components and explanations, not as a judgment about the player.

The decision assessment uses the expected conditions available when the month began. The outcome comparison shows what actually occurred. Keep the initial forecast intact when reviewing a month: replacing it with hindsight would make forecasting skill impossible to assess.

The headline is a **0–100 decision score** with a simple letter: **A ≥ 90, B ≥ 80, C ≥ 70, D ≥ 60, F < 60**. The score combines these dimensions:

| Dimension | Maximum points |
|---|---:|
| Earnings discipline | 25 |
| Cash and inventory discipline | 20 |
| Serving customers | 20 |
| Quality and future capability | 20 |
| Liquidity resilience | 15 |

Decision-quality rubric **2.0** anchors each dimension to a modeled operating standard. Seventy-five percent retains that criterion score; the remaining quarter starts from the same score and can be reduced for avoidable shortfalls against tested feasible policies. Comparison can only deduct points. Being the strongest of several poor plans cannot manufacture a high grade, and operating break-even earns half the earnings points before any comparison deduction. These are teaching priorities, not external benchmarks of executive competence. Borrowing alone does not create earnings or cash-plus-unused-credit resources.

The board separately asks whether actual results met its published goals. The decision coach asks how reasonable the chosen plan looked under the expected conditions available at the time. The scores can differ without contradiction. Historical coaching grades are recalculated under rubric 2.0, while saved decisions, accounts, and actual results remain unchanged. See the [coaching model](coaching-model.md) for the full rubric.

The hindsight comparison asks how alternative plans would have performed from the same opening position under the same realized conditions. **Best tested** means the strongest result among the plans the coach evaluated under its stated score. It is not a proof of a global optimum, advice to copy those decisions into a different month, or the best possible campaign strategy. Different goals and longer investment horizons can favor a different plan.

Open **What worked, what to change** after a month, or **Results → Coach** beside a past month. Inspect an alternative's actual decision changes as well as its expected and realized results. Historical comparisons require the stored campaign to reproduce the recorded results; if a modified or unsupported history cannot be replayed, coaching reports that limitation.

Useful questions after a round are: Did price cover the relevant costs? Did output match the dealer need? Was cash protected? Did quality and service hold up? Did a choice improve this month's score by sacrificing something valuable later? Did the market move, or did our position within it move?

## Understand the cycle and the surprise

The public market outlook describes a scenario's expected direction. Seasonality describes the pattern within the year. Realized shocks can move the actual month away from that expectation. These are distinct: an upswing can have a weak month, and a downcycle can have a seasonal rebound.

New cyclical campaigns start with a **Soft market**, **Flat market**, or **Upswing market** outlook. The forecast updates as observed conditions change; demand turns can persist across months, and supplier disruptions can take time to recover. A saved seed permits a reproducible campaign: the same model, opening state, seed, and decisions produce the same results. Reproducibility makes comparisons fair; changing the scenario seed creates a different challenge. Expected forecasts do not reveal future surprise outcomes.

Existing campaigns retain their original market rules and duration. Version 1.0.0 and 1.1.0 saves remain one year; a new three-year campaign uses version 1.2.0. Start a new campaign to select a different duration or market model, and export a backup first to retain the previous run. The [market-cycle model](market-cycles.md) and [three-year rules](multi-year.md) document the scenario and replay mechanics.

The game uses this structure to practice adjustment, not to teach that every downturn lasts a fixed number of months. Preserve options, watch channel inventory, and revisit assumptions after each result. Do not treat the annual outlook as a guarantee.

## Build earning capacity across years

A three-year campaign gives investments time to mature and produce observable results. The calendar continues through month 36: cash, debt, customer loans, inventory, equipment, quality, workforce capability, technology, and project pipelines all carry across December. There is no annual reset or cash grant. A factory commitment in November becomes available the following February; December engineering matures in March.

Capacity represents more production slots. Engineering represents better product capability in the demand model. Neither guarantees sales or a return. Costs arrive before operating benefits; fixed standard material and conversion costs do not fall automatically. Follow later deliveries, contribution, working capital, and collected cash to judge whether the added capability is useful.

Compare the working plan with the investment over the same months in the **Investment lab**, including **To campaign end** when the longer horizon is relevant. Recurring budgets repeat in the projection; new capex and corporate borrowing apply once. A policy that runs out of cash shortens the common comparison. Expected projections are scenarios, not proof of a payback period or future market value.

Seasonality repeats each year, but hidden shocks use new campaign months and can persist across annual boundaries. The opening market scenario gradually moves toward the flat reference after year one under an explicit synthetic rule; this is not a promise of recovery. The board's previously announced goals stay fixed. Reassess the operating response without treating future demand as guaranteed.

## Historical equipment-industry context

These dated examples explain why cycles and channel inventory belong in the game. They are not a statement about today's market:

- CNH reported **$24.7 billion of consolidated revenue in 2023, up 5%** from 2022. Its 2024 annual report records **$19.836 billion in 2024, down 19.7%**; Agriculture net sales fell **22.8%** to $14.007 billion. Company revenue can change sharply between years, and it is not the same measure as industry retail units. [CNH's 2023 results](https://media.cnh.com/emea/cnh/cnh-industrial-n.v.-reports-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2023-results/s/69bb93ad-675a-48e1-9eff-dce6c9c4d030) and [2024 Form 10-K, pp. 50–51](https://www.cnh.com/-/media/CNHi/cnhicorporate/Investor-relation/financial_information/annual_reports/2024/CNH-2024-10-K.pdf?rev=-1).
- CNH said Agriculture dealer inventory fell by **over $700 million in Q4 2024**, accompanied by **34% fewer production hours**. Cutting factory output can be a deliberate response to excess channel stock. [CNH's 2024 results, released February 4, 2025](https://media.cnh.com/ASIA-PACIFIC-ENGLISH/cnh/cnh-industrial-n.v.-reports-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2024-results/s/f5b51dd6-aa53-4d1c-bcbf-6db7ca015350).
- In 2024, CNH reported North American industry unit volumes falling **16% for tractors over 140 hp** and **22% for combines**. Asia Pacific combine demand rose **11%**. A weak aggregate market can contain different regional and product conditions. The annual report also identifies farm income, commodity prices, financing, and weather as demand influences. [2024 Form 10-K, pp. 5–7, 46 and 50–51](https://www.cnh.com/-/media/CNHi/cnhicorporate/Investor-relation/financial_information/annual_reports/2024/CNH-2024-10-K.pdf?rev=-1).

The first practice goal is to explain a decision before making it, then explain the difference between the forecast and the result afterward. Better business judgment comes from understanding that difference across several rounds.
